The Reverend Arthur Pumphrey had owned the bicycle for thirty years and ridden it twice.
Every Sunday after evensong he polished it with the reverence other men reserve for their wives, and with rather more attention.
The bicycle stood in the vestry between the font and the umbrella stand, and the parish had long since stopped asking whether it was a vehicle or a relic.
Mrs. Higgs, who arranged the flowers, maintained that it was the only thing in Little Wending that had never once gossiped.
Pumphrey himself held that a bicycle, like a sermon, improved with keeping.
On the first Tuesday in March a letter arrived from the Bishop, sealed in purple wax and smelling faintly of cathedral.
"My dear Pumphrey," it began, "it has come to our attention that you are the last incumbent in the diocese who visits his parishioners on foot.
"We regard this as a spiritual achievement of the highest order, and as a logistical catastrophe.
"The Archdeacon reports that by the time you reach the outlying farms, the sick have frequently recovered and the dying have lost patience.
"Kindly acquire some means of conveyance before Easter, or at the very least a faster horse. Yours in Christ, Cuthbert, Bishop of Wendham."
Pumphrey read the letter three times, then went to consult the bicycle.
The bicycle, as usual, said nothing, which Pumphrey took for agreement.
That evening he wheeled it out of the vestry and into the lane. The light was going, and the hedges were full of the small sounds of birds settling for the night.
He had forgotten how high the saddle was. For a long moment he stood beside it with one hand on the bell, as a man stands at the edge of cold water.
Then he was moving. The lane fell away towards the river, and the wind came up off the water and pressed against his coat.
He passed the Higgses' cottage, the dark bulk of the mill, and the field where he had once buried old Tom Barrow's dog for a boy who could not bear to do it himself.
At the bridge he stopped and listened to the river. He thought of his father, who had given him the bicycle, and who had never once told him to hurry.
When he came back it was full dark. He leaned the bicycle against the vestry wall and stood for some time with his hand on the cold handlebars.
He did not polish it that night.
In the morning he wrote to the Bishop.
His reply ran to eleven pages and was later described by the Archdeacon as the longest refusal in the history of the diocese that contained no actual refusal.
In it he agreed to acquire a conveyance, provided that it could be got into the vestry, did not frighten the bellringers, and had been personally blessed by its previous owner.
The Bishop, who had a motor car, a chauffeur, and no previous owner to speak of, sent a curate.
The curate was twenty-four, owned a motorcycle, and believed sincerely that God had made the hills for the purpose of being taken at speed.
Within a week he had visited every farm in the parish, frightened every bellringer, and been asked by three separate widows whether he was the Second Coming or merely in a hurry for it.
Mrs. Higgs said he was a credit to the Church, and then went round the village saying a good deal else.
By Easter the parishioners had divided into those who wanted the curate to stay and those who wanted to survive.
Pumphrey, for his part, took to riding the bicycle on Tuesdays, slowly and not very far.
At the Easter service the Bishop asked him how the new arrangement suited him.
"Admirably," said Pumphrey. "The curate visits the sick, and I visit the curate's victims."
